# Getting started

What this course is based on, and how to read the examples.

## The name Kullilli
id: name-kullilli
grammar: Introduction › Spelling of the name Kullilli, p. 4

Books about Australian languages spell the name of the language in several ways, for example *Garlali* and *Kalali*. Austlang, the list of First languages of Australia kept by AIATSIS (austlang.aiatsis.gov.au), gives a few more spellings and more information about sources.

These lessons spell it **Kullilli**. This is the spelling the community prefers, it helps English speakers say the name the right way, and it is widely known.

Q: Name two other spellings of Kullilli that you might find in older books.
A: *Garlali* and *Kalali*.

Q: Give one reason these lessons use the spelling Kullilli.
A: It is the community's preferred spelling. (It also helps English speakers say the name correctly, and it is widely known.)

Q: Where can you find more spellings of the name and more about Kullilli sources?
A: In Austlang, the AIATSIS list of First languages of Australia.

## Where our Kullilli comes from
id: sources
grammar: Introduction › About Kullilli sources, p. 4–5

What we know about Kullilli comes from records made with Kullilli speakers. There are six main sources:

- Gavan Breen's interviews with Paddy Ardoch and George Wallace, in the 1960s.
- Nils Holmer's interviews with Paddy Ardoch, in the 1950s.
- Stephen Wurm and Maryalice McDonald's interviews with Charlie Phillips.
- Interviews with Charlie Phillips and Janet Mathews.
- Luise Hercus and Claire Bowern's interviews with Peter Hood, in 1999.
- Smaller word lists, such as those in *Science of Man* and in Curr's *The Australian Race* (1886).

Some sources have a lot of information; others are mostly lists of words. These lessons use all of them, but they mostly follow the Kullilli of **Charlie Phillips**, as recorded by McDonald and Wurm, because that is the largest source and has the most sentences.

Q: How many main sources of Kullilli are there?
A: Six.

Q: Whose Kullilli do these lessons mostly follow, and why?
A: Charlie Phillips's, as recorded by McDonald and Wurm, because it is the largest source with the most sentences.

Q: Which speaker did Luise Hercus and Claire Bowern work with in 1999?
A: Peter Hood.

## What the records leave out
id: gaps-and-variation
grammar: Introduction › About Kullilli sources, p. 5

None of the sources has a very large vocabulary. They concentrate on plants, animals, basic actions and everyday words. Very few words for feelings or abstract ideas were written down. That is not because Kullilli people didn't talk about these things: the records are just not a complete record of the language.

Other things we talk about today didn't exist (or weren't common) in the 1950s and 1960s, such as mobile phones. There are no Kullilli words for them yet, but new words can be made. All languages make new words when new things come into use, and Kullilli is no different.

People also spoke Kullilli in different ways, just as people in America, England and Australia speak English differently. Some differences were in pronunciation and some were in the choice of words. The dictionary has more about which words different speakers used.

Q: Why are there so few Kullilli words for feelings in the records?
A: Not because people didn't talk about feelings. The records are not a complete record of the language.

Q: Can Kullilli have words for new things like mobile phones?
A: Yes. All languages make new words when new things come into use; Kullilli words for these still need to be made.

Q: True or false: everyone spoke Kullilli in exactly the same way.
A: False. There were differences in pronunciation and in choice of words, just as there are in English.

## Reading the examples
id: reading-examples
grammar: Introduction › Reading Kullilli examples, p. 6

Each Kullilli example has three lines. The first line is the Kullilli. The second line gives the English for each Kullilli word, right underneath it. The third line is a translation into everyday English.

```ex
nganytya ngarnidya dhika-gala wikarla
my       father    go.back-is tomorrow
My father is coming back tomorrow.
```

So {nganytya} means ‘my’, {ngarnidya} means ‘father’, and {wikarla} means ‘tomorrow’. A dash separates a word from its endings: {dhika-gala} is {dhika} ‘go back’ with the ending {-gala}. When one Kullilli part needs several English words, they are joined with dots (*go.back*).

Under some examples you will see a number like *Recorded sentence 146/18*: this is where the sentence comes from in the collection of recorded sentences. Examples marked *Made up by the guide's authors* were built from what we know about how Kullilli works; they were not recorded from a speaker.

Q: What are the three lines of an example?
A: The Kullilli; the English for each Kullilli word; and a translation into everyday English.

Q: In the example, which word means ‘father’?
A: {ngarnidya}.

Q: What does it mean when an example is marked "made up"?
A: The guide's authors built it from what we know about Kullilli. It was not recorded from a speaker.

## How Kullilli words work
id: word-parts
grammar: Types of Kullilli words, p. 17

In English, most words mean something on their own. In Kullilli, a word is often made of several parts, and each part adds some meaning.

So Kullilli often uses one word where English uses more than one. English says *from Brisbane*, but Kullilli says {Brisbane-andru}: the ending {-andru} means ‘from’.

The order of words is different too. Kullilli verbs tend to come at the end of the sentence (though they don't have to), while English verbs go in the middle.

These lessons start with greetings and numbers, so you can start talking straight away, and then go on to verbs, nouns, pronouns, and the other kinds of words.

Q: How do you say ‘from Brisbane’ in Kullilli?
A: {Brisbane-andru}.

Q: Which part of {Brisbane-andru} means ‘from’?
A: {-andru}.

Q: Where do Kullilli verbs tend to go in a sentence?
A: At the end, though they don't have to.
