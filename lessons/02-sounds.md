# Sounds and spelling

How to read and say Kullilli words. Kullilli is written with English letters, but some letters (and pairs of letters) stand for different sounds, and some Kullilli sounds don't exist in English. You can listen to recordings of words in the dictionary while you practise. If you'd rather start talking straight away, you can skip ahead to Greetings and come back here later.

## Three vowels: a, i, u
id: vowels
grammar: The Kullilli Alphabet & Sounds › Vowels, p. 7

English has lots of vowel sounds, but Kullilli has only three: **a**, **i** and **u**.

| Letter | How to say it | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| a | like the *u* in *but* (not the *a* in *apple*) | {gala} ‘hey’, {waka} ‘tree’, {yara} ‘message’ |
| i | like the *i* in *bit* (not the *i* in *I'm* or *idle*) | {girri} ‘stomach’, {mirri} ‘dog’, {wiyi} ‘fire’ |
| u | like the *u* in *put* or the *oo* in *look* (not the *u* in *umbrella* or *cup*) | {bunu} ‘riverbank’, {guturu} ‘swan’, {muthu} ‘boy, kids’ |

Q: How many vowel sounds does Kullilli have, and what are they?
A: Three: a, i and u.

Q: Is the *a* in {gala} ‘hey’ said like the *a* in *apple*?
A: No. It is like the *u* in *but*.

Q: Which English word has a vowel like Kullilli *u*: *put* or *cup*?
A: *put*.

## The easiest consonants: b, p, g, k, m, w, y
id: easy-consonants
grammar: The Kullilli Alphabet & Sounds › “Easiest” consonants, p. 8

Seven Kullilli consonant letters sound the same as, or very like, the same letters in English.

| Letter | How to say it | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| b | as in *baby* | {bala} ‘today’, {babadya} ‘husband’ |
| p | as in *spy* | {kapi} ‘egg’ |
| g | as in *gag* | {galga} ‘evening’, {gula} ‘grass’ |
| k | as in *sky* | {kunduru} ‘black bream (fish)’, {galka} ‘strike’ |
| m | as in *moon* | {mala} ‘hand’, {gumi} ‘big’ |
| w | as in *wow* | {waliba} ‘help’, {gawa} ‘come’ |
| y | as in *yes* (never as in *why* or *happy*) | {yulu} ‘truthfully’, {yanggu} ‘spear’ |

Q: Which seven Kullilli consonants sound (almost) the same as in English?
A: b, p, g, k, m, w and y.

Q: Is Kullilli *y* ever a vowel, as in English *happy*?
A: No. It is always like the *y* in *yes*.

Q: What does {gawa} mean?
A: ‘come’.

## One sound, two letters: ng
id: ng
grammar: The Kullilli Alphabet & Sounds › “Mostly easy” consonants, p. 8–9

**ng** is a single sound, like the *ng* in *sing*. It feels a bit like *n*, but with your tongue bunched up near the back of your mouth.

Unlike English, Kullilli **ng** can come at the beginning of a word.

```words
ngapa | water
manga | ears
```

Q: How many sounds does *ng* stand for?
A: One.

Q: How is Kullilli *ng* different from English *ng*?
A: It can come at the beginning of a word, as in {ngapa} ‘water’.

Q: What does {ngapa} mean?
A: ‘water’.

## Two r sounds: r and rr
id: r-and-rr
grammar: The Kullilli Alphabet & Sounds › “Mostly easy” consonants, p. 8–9; The consonant sound rr, p. 12

Kullilli has more than one r-like sound.

A single **r** can be said in two ways, and both are sounds of Australian English: like the *r* in *rat*, or like the quick *tt* in *butter* (said fast, with an Australian accent). You may just need to learn for each word which way its *r* is said.

```words
wara | who
bari | think
```

A double **rr** is a different sound: a rolled or trilled *r*, like the *r* in Scottish English, Italian or Spanish.

```words
burra | feather
murra | camp
garri | fish
```

Q: What are the two ways a single *r* can be said?
A: Like the *r* in *rat*, or like the quick *tt* in *butter*.

Q: How is *rr* said?
A: As a rolled (trilled) *r*, as in Scottish English, Italian or Spanish.

Q: Are *r* and *rr* the same sound?
A: No, they are different sounds.

## d, t, n, l, and the start of a word
id: d-t-n-l
grammar: The Kullilli Alphabet & Sounds › “Mostly easy” consonants, p. 8–9

Inside a word, **d**, **t**, **n** and **l** sound much as they do in Australian English (as in *adore*, *atop*, *another*, *alone*).

```words
manda | hold
miti | dirty
ini | you
mala | hand
```

But when a word **begins** with *d*, *t*, *n* or *l*, the sound changes: it is said with the tip of the tongue curled back, as if the word were spelled with *rd*, *rt*, *rn* or *rl* (see the lesson on curled-back sounds). For example, {duna} ‘time’ is said as if it were spelled *rduna*.

This doesn't apply to words beginning with *dy, dh, ty, th, ny, nh, ly* or *lh*: those are sounds of their own (see the next lessons).

Q: How is *d* said at the start of a word?
A: Like *rd*, with the tip of the tongue curled back.

Q: How is {duna} ‘time’ pronounced?
A: As if it were spelled *rduna*.

Q: Does the rule apply to a word beginning with *dh*?
A: No. *dh* (like *dy, th, ty, nh, ny, lh, ly*) keeps its own sound.

## Tongue between the teeth: dh, th, nh, lh
id: dental-sounds
grammar: The Kullilli Alphabet & Sounds › Dental consonants, p. 10

**dh**, **th**, **nh** and **lh** are each one sound, even though they are written with two letters (like *ch* in English). They are like *d*, *t*, *n* and *l*, but with the tip of your tongue **between your front teeth**. Each has an *h*: think of the *h* as a reminder to put your tongue between your teeth.

Kullilli **th** is not English *th*. The air stops completely before you let the sound go, so it sounds closer to English *t* than to English *th*.

| Letter | How to say it | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| dh | like *d*, tongue between the teeth | {dhuna} ‘time’, {niladha} ‘yesterday’ |
| th | like *t*, tongue between the teeth | {guthi} ‘run’, {nguthi} ‘meat’ |
| nh | like *n*, tongue between the teeth | {nhadya} ‘see’, {wanhdhu} ‘often’ |
| lh | like *l*, tongue between the teeth | {gulha} ‘sit’, {walhtha} ‘take’ |

Saying these sounds may be easier than hearing the difference between them and *d, t, n, l*. Listening to recordings in the dictionary helps.

Q: Where does your tongue go for *dh*, *th*, *nh* and *lh*?
A: The tip goes between your front teeth.

Q: Is Kullilli *th* like the *th* in English *thin*?
A: No. The air stops completely, so it sounds closer to English *t*.

Q: What does the *h* in these letters remind you to do?
A: Put the tip of your tongue between your teeth.

## Middle of the tongue: dy, ty, ny, ly
id: palatal-sounds
grammar: The Kullilli Alphabet & Sounds › Palatal consonants, p. 10–11

**dy**, **ty**, **ny** and **ly** are each one sound too. You say them with the **middle** of your tongue (not the tip) pressed against the roof of your mouth.

| Letter | How to say it | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| dy | like *j* in *jar* or *d* in *dew*, but softer | {dyilpa} ‘dew’, {nganydya} ‘want’ |
| ty | like *ch* in *choose*, *t* in *tune*, or *tch* in *catch*, but softer, with less air | {nganytya} ‘my’, {yibi buntyu} ‘eyebrows’ |
| ny | like *ny* in *canyon*, *gn* in *lasagna*, or *n* in *onion* | {bindrinya} ‘grasshopper’, {bunytyu} ‘hair, fur’ |
| ly | like *ll* in *million* | {galyi} ‘far’, {walya} ‘no’ |

Remember that *y* is not a vowel in Kullilli. So *dy* is never said as in *ready*, *ty* never as in *beauty*, *ny* never as in *many*, and *ly* never as in *rally*.

Q: Which part of the tongue touches the roof of the mouth for *dy*, *ty*, *ny* and *ly*?
A: The middle of the tongue (not the tip).

Q: Is the *ny* in {bindrinya} ‘grasshopper’ said like the *ny* in *many*?
A: No. It is like the *ny* in *canyon*.

Q: Which English word has a sound like Kullilli *ly*?
A: *million* (the *ll*).

## Tongue curled back: rd, rt, rn, rl
id: retroflex-sounds
grammar: The Kullilli Alphabet & Sounds › Retroflex consonants, p. 11–12

For **rd**, **rt**, **rn** and **rl**, curl the tip of your tongue up and back until the underside of your tongue just touches the roof of your mouth. Then say *d*, *t*, *n* or *l*. The tongue position is a bit like the one for the *r* in English *rat*.

| Letter | How to say it | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| rd | like *d*, tongue tip curled back | {burdu} ‘windbreak’, {gardra} ‘spear’ |
| rt | like *t*, tongue tip curled back | {birta} ‘night’, {warta} ‘top of leg, thigh’ |
| rn | like *n*, tongue tip curled back | {warnpa} ‘mud’, {barna} ‘goanna’ |
| rl | like *l*, tongue tip curled back | {durlku} ‘heart’, {gurlu} ‘knee’ |

You can probably feel the difference from *d, t, n, l* more easily than you can hear it. And remember: a word that **starts** with *d, t, n* or *l* is said with these curled-back sounds.

Q: What do you do with your tongue for *rd*, *rt*, *rn* and *rl*?
A: Curl the tip up and back so the underside touches the roof of your mouth.

Q: Which curled-back sound is in {barna} ‘goanna’?
A: *rn*.

Q: Which words are said with a curled-back sound even though they're not spelled with *r*?
A: Words that start with *d*, *t*, *n* or *l*.

## All the sounds at a glance
id: sound-chart
grammar: The Kullilli Alphabet & Sounds › Summary of Kullilli sounds, p. 12–13

The vowels are simple: **a**, **i**, **u**. This chart shows all the consonants. The top row says where in the mouth the sound is made; the left column says how it is made.

| | lips | teeth | gum ridge | roof of mouth | tongue curled back | back of mouth |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| stops (voiced) | b | dh | d | dy | rd | g |
| stops (voiceless) | p | th | t | ty | rt | k |
| nasals | m | nh | n | ny | rn | ng |
| l-sounds | | lh | l | ly | rl | |
| r-sounds | | | r, rr | | | |
| glides | w | | | y | | |

Linguists call these places *bilabial* (lips), *dental* (teeth), *alveolar* (gum ridge), *palatal* (roof of mouth), *retroflex* (curled back) and *velar* (back of mouth).

> Remember: *d, t, n, l* at the start of a word are said like *rd, rt, rn, rl*; and *r* can be said like the *r* in *rat* or the *tt* in *butter*.

Q: Which column do *dh*, *th*, *nh* and *lh* belong to?
A: The teeth (dental) column.

Q: Which sounds are made at the back of the mouth?
A: *g*, *k* and *ng*.

Q: Which nasal sound goes with *dy* and *ty*?
A: *ny*.

## The dash: roots and endings
id: dash
grammar: The Kullilli Alphabet & Sounds › Using the dash ‘-’, p. 13–14

The dash (**-**) is not a letter. It separates a root word from its endings, a bit like the dash in English *non-traditional* or *semi-circle*, but Kullilli uses it much more: in these lessons, every ending is separated from the word it goes on.

For example, {marpa} means ‘cook’ and {-rra} is the future ending. Together they make {marpa-rra} ‘will cook’.

As your Kullilli improves, you may not need the dashes any more. Write them or leave them out, whichever you prefer: they make the parts of words easier to see.

Q: Is the dash a letter of the Kullilli alphabet?
A: No. It separates a root word from its endings.

Q: Put {marpa} ‘cook’ and {-rra} (future) together. What does it mean?
A: {marpa-rra} ‘will cook’.

Q: Do you have to write the dashes?
A: No, it's up to you.

## Stress: always the first syllable
id: stress
grammar: The Kullilli Alphabet & Sounds › Stress, p. 14

*Stress* is the syllable said with the most emphasis. In English it can fall anywhere: TELevision, anniVERsary, underSTAND.

Kullilli is much easier: stress is **always on the first syllable**.

```words
nganytya | my
niladha | yesterday
barna | goanna
```

Go back over the words in the earlier lessons and practise putting the emphasis on the first part of each word.

Q: Which syllable is stressed in Kullilli words?
A: The first.

Q: Where is the stress in {wikarla} ‘tomorrow’?
A: On the first syllable, *wi*.

Q: Is Kullilli stress like English stress?
A: No. English stress can fall on different syllables; Kullilli stress is always on the first.

## Why old sources spell words differently
id: old-spellings
grammar: The Kullilli Alphabet & Sounds › Spelling in different Kullilli sources, p. 14–15

You don't need to learn other spellings to speak Kullilli, but if you read the older sources you will meet them.

- The missionaries and older sources (before about the 1940s) spelled words as if they were English. They weren't consistent, they missed details that matter in Aboriginal languages, and what they wrote can often be read in several ways.
- Some later sources are very precise about every detail of the sounds, with many special symbols. This helps us work out what the older sources meant.
- Other sources write only the differences in sound that change meaning.

Holmer's (1988) description of Paddy Ardoch's Kullilli is mostly consistent. Breen is fairly regular and adds extra detail. Tindale adds a lot of detail but usually isn't very accurate. McDonald and Wurm's record of Charlie Phillips is the most consistent, and the largest: over 500 sentences.

Q: How did the older sources (before the 1940s) spell Kullilli words?
A: As if they were English, inconsistently and without important details.

Q: Which source is the most consistent and the largest?
A: McDonald and Wurm's record of Charlie Phillips (over 500 sentences).

Q: Do you need to learn the old spellings to speak Kullilli?
A: No, but they are useful if you read the older sources.
