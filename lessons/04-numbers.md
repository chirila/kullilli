# Numbers and amounts

Counting from one to four, and words for ‘all’ and ‘many’.

## One and two
id: one-two
grammar: Numbers › Numerals 1–4, p. 22

The Kullilli records we have only include the numbers one to four. We can't say for sure whether there were only words up to four, or whether there were more that nobody wrote down.

```words
dharrany | one
-watyu | one, a
barrkulu | two
```

There are two ways to say ‘one’. {dharrany} can be used on its own, but {-watyu} must be attached to another word, as in {garna-watyu} ‘one man, a man’.

Q: What is the Kullilli for ‘two’?
A: {barrkulu}.

Q: What are the two ways to say ‘one’, and how are they different?
A: {dharrany}, which stands on its own, and {-watyu}, which is attached to another word.

Q: Which numbers do the Kullilli records include?
A: One to four.

## -watyu: ‘one’ or ‘a’
id: watyu
grammar: Numbers › Numerals 1–4, p. 22–23

{-watyu} is often translated as ‘one’, but also as ‘a’ or ‘an’. In English, *a man* means any one man, while *the man* means a particular man. {-watyu} sometimes has this ‘any one’ meaning, like English *a*.

```ex 146/18
garna-watyu nhani-guru    waga-gala   walbiri-lunga
man-one     3sg.nom-there lay-present shade-locative
A man (that one) is lying in the shade.
```

```ex 115/51
manhdha-rra  ngathu  ingka   makurra-watyu-anha
grasp-future 1sg.erg 2sg.dat stick-one-accusative
I'll pick up a stick, that one, for you.
```

Q: What does {garna-watyu} mean?
A: ‘one man’ or ‘a man’.

Q: Can {-watyu} be used as a word on its own?
A: No, it has to be attached to another word.

Q: In 115/51, which word means ‘a stick’?
A: {makurra-watyu-anha}.

## Three and four
id: three-four
grammar: Numbers › Numerals 1–4, p. 22

Once you know {dharrany} ‘one’ and {barrkulu} ‘two’, three and four are easy: Kullilli makes them from one and two.

```words
barrkulu dharrany | three (literally ‘two one’)
barrkulu barrkulu | four (literally ‘two two’)
```

Q: How do you say ‘three’?
A: {barrkulu dharrany}.

Q: What does {barrkulu barrkulu} mean, word for word, and what number is it?
A: ‘two two’, which means four.

Q: Which two numbers are used to make three and four?
A: One ({dharrany}) and two ({barrkulu}).

## Number systems in Australian languages
id: number-systems
grammar: Numbers › A note on numbers, p. 23

Some languages have numbers you can keep combining as far as you like. Others have numbers up to five or ten, or sometimes none at all. Australia's Indigenous languages vary a lot: some had no numbers that we know of, some had a few (one, two and three, for example), and some had numbers you could count with without limit.

Think about how often you actually use a big number like *one thousand, three hundred and fifty-four*: probably not very often.

Some Aboriginal communities have made new numbers. Warlpiri, for example, now has a word for seven (from the word for a boomerang shaped like a 7); eight is a double seed pod; and nine is a cup (a cup with a handle, seen from above, looks a bit like a 9).

Q: Did all Aboriginal languages have the same kind of number system?
A: No. Some had no numbers we know of, some had a few, and some could count without limit.

Q: How did Warlpiri make a word for seven?
A: From the word for a boomerang shaped like a 7.

Q: Can communities make new number words?
A: Yes. Warlpiri, for example, has new words for seven, eight and nine.

## Counting things
id: counting
grammar: Numbers › Numerals 1–4, p. 22–23

To count things, the number usually comes **before** the noun:

```words
dharrany garna | one person
barrkulu garna | two people
barrkulu dharrany garna | three people
barrkulu barrkulu garna | four people
```

To use {-watyu} for ‘one’ instead, say {garna-watyu} ‘one person’.

English adds *-s* when there is more than one: one dog, two dogs. In Kullilli (as in many languages of the world) you can just use the number without changing anything else: {dharrany thithi} ‘one dog’, {barrkulu thithi} ‘two dogs’ (literally ‘two dog’).

Q: Where does the number usually go: before or after the noun?
A: Before the noun.

Q: How do you say ‘three people’?
A: {barrkulu dharrany garna}.

Q: Does the word for ‘dog’ change in ‘two dogs’?
A: No: {barrkulu thithi}, literally ‘two dog’.

## All and many: buka and wandru
id: buka-wandru
grammar: Numbers › Non-numeric amounts, p. 23–24

English also talks about amounts without numbers, with words like *some*, *many* and *all*. Kullilli has:

```words
buka | all
wandru | much, many, a lot
```

{buka} ‘all’ doesn't take endings; it is always used on its own, most often after the noun. {wandru} can be used on its own:

```ex 82/18
wandru yanhdha-dhika-gala
lot    go-go.back-present
A mob is coming.
```

{wandru} can also take endings, such as {-ani}. Here {wandru} and {buka} are used together:

```ex 213/21
wandru-ani     dhana garna-ani     girni-ani        buka
lot-nominative 3pl   man-nominative strong-nominative all
The mob of men (together) are strong.
```

Q: What does {buka} mean?
A: ‘all’.

Q: Can {buka} take endings?
A: No, it is always used on its own.

Q: What does {wandru} mean, and can it take endings?
A: ‘much, many, a lot’. Yes, it can, as in {wandru-ani}.

## Two of something: -bula
id: two-bula
grammar: Numbers › Non-numeric amounts, p. 24

When you talk about how many people or things there are, the number can come before or after the noun. When you talk about two things, the noun can also have the ending {-bula}, which means ‘two of them’ (linguists call this *dual*).

```ex 79/15
barrkulu dhithi-bula
two      dog-dual
Two dogs.
```

In this sentence ‘two’ comes after the pronoun {ngali} ‘the two of us’:

```ex 383/63
ngali-ngu    barkulu-lu   gangga-rra   nhanha-guru
1dl-ergative two-ergative carry-future 3sg.acc-there
The two of us will carry this one.
```

Q: What does {-bula} add to a noun?
A: It shows there are two (dual).

Q: How do you say ‘two dogs’ using {-bula}?
A: {barrkulu dhithi-bula}.

Q: In 383/63, does ‘two’ come before or after the word for ‘the two of us’?
A: After.
