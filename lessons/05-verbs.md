# Verbs and time

Verbs are words for actions or states: in English, *run*, *is* and *talk* are verbs. English adds *-s*, *-ed* or *will* to a verb to show when something happens. Kullilli adds endings to the verb.

## Now: -gala
id: present-gala
grammar: Verbs › Present: -gala, p. 25

To talk about what is happening now, add the ending {-gala} to the verb. This is called the *present tense*.

```words
bari-gala | thinking
bunhthi-gala | coming
dhithi-gala | laughing
dunda-gala | growing
marnnga-gala | making
ngara-gala | hearing
ngutya-gala | giving
ngulka-gala | learning
```

Q: Which ending do you add to a verb to say something is happening now?
A: {-gala}.

Q: What does {ngara-gala} mean?
A: ‘hearing’.

Q: {marpa} means ‘cook’. How do you say ‘cooking’?
A: {marpa-gala}.

## Verbs can go anywhere
id: present-sentences
grammar: Verbs › Present: -gala, p. 25

In English, the verb usually comes after the subject (the person or thing doing the action). Kullilli verbs can come at the beginning, in the middle, or at the end of a sentence. These sentences are from Charlie Phillips:

```ex
dhithi-gala   nhaniguru garnaani
laugh-present that      man
That man is laughing.
```

```ex 99/35
gawalyalu marnnga-gala bakarranyi
uncle     make-present boomerang
Uncle is making a boomerang.
```

```ex 71/7
ngathu yulanha ngutya-gala
I.erg  you     give-present
I give it to you.
```

Q: In ‘That man is laughing’, where is the verb?
A: At the beginning: {dhithi-gala}.

Q: Can a Kullilli verb come at the end of a sentence?
A: Yes. It can come at the beginning, in the middle, or at the end.

Q: What does {gawalyalu marnnga-gala bakarranyi} mean?
A: ‘Uncle is making a boomerang.’

## The past: two endings
id: past-endings
grammar: Verbs › Past, p. 26

For things that have already happened (the *past tense*), Kullilli has two endings: {-nga} and {-gali}.

```words
bunhthi-nga | came
bura-nga | told
burli-nga | fell
garki-nga | called
guthi-nga | ran
ngalka-nga | thought
ngulka-nga | learned
```

```words
bunhthi-gali | came
bura-gali | told
gatyi-gali | broke
gunka-gali | asked
mama-gali | stole
mukari-gali | forgot
nadya-gali | looked
yantha-gali | went
```

Q: What are the two past endings?
A: {-nga} and {-gali}.

Q: Give two ways to say ‘came’.
A: {bunhthi-nga} and {bunhthi-gali}.

Q: What does {mama-gali} mean?
A: ‘stole’.

## Finished actions: -nga
id: past-nga
grammar: Verbs › Past 1: -nga, p. 26

The difference between the two past endings is subtle. Generally, {-nga} is used for **completed** actions. So {guthi-nga} means ‘ran’ or ‘have run, had run’. It can't mean an action that was still going on, like ‘was running’ or ‘had been running’.

```ex
guthi-nga nhaniguru warliwani
run-past  that      child
That child ran.
```

```ex 1
dhanagurungu muraba-nga  gungaanha
all.of.them  finish-past food
All of them have finished this food.
```

Q: What kind of past action is {-nga} used for?
A: Completed (finished) actions.

Q: Can {guthi-nga} mean ‘was running’?
A: No. It means ‘ran’ or ‘have/had run’.

Q: How do you say ‘That child ran’?
A: {guthi-nga nhaniguru warliwani}.

## Ongoing actions in the past: -gali
id: past-gali
grammar: Verbs › Past 2: -gali, p. 27

{-gali} can be used for actions that were **going on** in the past. So {bunhthi-gali} can mean ‘came’ or ‘was coming’, but not ‘have come’ or ‘had come’.

```ex
mukarri-gali nganyi nungka yarrinya
forget-past  I.nom  his    name
I forgot his name.
```

```ex made-up
dhithi-gali nhaniguru garnaani
laugh-past  that      man
That man was laughing.
```

```ex
gari nadya-gali gulha yundru yalairri garnaani yanhtha-dhika-gali
hey  see-past   sit   you    there    man      go-go.back-past
Hey, when you were looking over there, did a man go past?
```

Q: Can {bunhthi-gali} mean ‘was coming’?
A: Yes. It can mean ‘came’ or ‘was coming’.

Q: How would you say ‘That man was laughing’?
A: {dhithi-gali nhaniguru garnaani}.

Q: What does {mukarri-gali nganyi nungka yarrinya} mean?
A: ‘I forgot his name.’

## -nga or -gali?
id: nga-or-gali
grammar: Verbs › Difference between -nga and -gali, p. 27

Don't worry too much about which to use. For a simple past event, either ending should work, and often the meaning seems the same. The guide's authors think {-gali} is used when there is a **time word** in the sentence, such as {niladha} ‘yesterday’. So even when an action is finished (which should mean {-nga}), you can use {-gali} if there's a time word.

```ex
bunhthi-nga ngalingala       nhaniguru garnaani
come-past   to.the.two.of.us that      man
That man came to the two of us.
```

```ex
nganytya ngarnidya bunhthi-gali niladha
my       father    come-past    yesterday
My father came yesterday.
```

```ex
gunka-nga ngathu nhinha
ask-past  I.erg  him
I asked him.
```

```ex
gunka-gali ngathu nhinha niladha
ask-past   I.erg  him    yesterday
I asked him yesterday.
```

Q: Which past ending seems to be used when there is a time word like {niladha} ‘yesterday’?
A: {-gali}.

Q: How do you say ‘I asked him yesterday’?
A: {gunka-gali ngathu nhinha niladha}.

Q: For a simple past event, does it matter much which ending you use?
A: Not much: either should work.

## The future: -rra
id: future-rra
grammar: Verbs › Future: -rra, p. 28

To talk about the future, use {-rra}. It doesn't matter how near or far in the future: you can use it for anything that happens after you talk about it. {-rra} means ‘will’ or ‘going to’: if {dhaltha} means ‘eat’, {dhaltha-rra} means ‘will eat’.

```words
barnrdi-rra | will cross
dhaltha-rra | will eat
dumpa-rra | will show
galka-rra | will strike
gulha-rra | will sit
gulpa-rra | will light
guthi-rra | will run
manhdha-rra | will get
marpa-rra | will cook
ngartandi-rra | will jump
ngudya-rra | will give
waga-rra | will lay
yanhtha-rra | will go
```

Q: What does the ending {-rra} mean?
A: ‘will’ or ‘going to’ (future).

Q: Does it matter how far in the future the event is?
A: No.

Q: How do you say ‘will run’?
A: {guthi-rra}.

## The future in sentences
id: future-sentences
grammar: Verbs › Future: -rra, p. 28

Again, the verb can come at the beginning, in the middle or at the end of the sentence.

```ex 115/51
manhdha-rra  ngathu ingka makurra-watyu-anha
grasp-future I      you   stick-one-accusative
I'll pick up a stick, that one, for you.
```

```ex
ga  nganyi yanhtha-rra ingala
yes I      go-future   you
Yes, I will go to you.
```

```ex 151/23
wiyinha bari  ngalingu gulpa-rra
fire    think we       light-future
I think we will light a fire.
```

Q: What does {ga nganyi yanhtha-rra ingala} mean?
A: ‘Yes, I will go to you.’

Q: In 151/23, which word is the verb with the future ending?
A: {gulpa-rra} ‘will light’.

Q: Where is the verb in 115/51?
A: At the beginning: {manhdha-rra}.

## More than time: other verb endings
id: mood-endings
grammar: Verbs › Other verb endings, p. 29

Verbs can tell us more than *when* something happens. A speaker might want to say how likely or certain something is, or how they feel about it. English does this with separate words like *should*, *must* and *can*: *She should/must/can do her homework.*

Kullilli does it with other verb endings. They go on the verb **instead of** a tense ending: you use either a tense ending like {-gali}, or one of these.

- {-rri}: might happen, or someone wishes it would (*irrealis*)
- {-lha}: will certainly happen, or is about to (*factive*)
- {-langu} or {-langa}: could happen, or someone is able to do it (*possibility*)

Q: Can a verb have both {-gali} and {-rri}?
A: No. These endings go on the verb instead of the tense endings.

Q: How does English usually show meanings like ‘should’ or ‘can’?
A: With separate words such as *should*, *must* and *can*.

Q: Which ending shows that something will certainly happen?
A: {-lha}.

## Might happen: -rri
id: irrealis-rri
grammar: Verbs › Irrealis: -rri, p. 29–30

*Irrealis* is a Latin word meaning ‘unreal’: things that won't happen, didn't happen, or aren't certain. Kullilli uses the ending {-rri} when talking about wishes, things we think might happen, and things we aren't sure about.

```ex 282/26
yundru  ngutya-rri     ngali-nha
you.erg give-irrealis 1dl-accusative
You (might) give to the two of us.
```

The person listening will probably give something to the two speakers, but it isn't guaranteed.

```ex 199/7
ngutya-lha   ngathu  inha    bakarranyi yundru  nganha  ngutya-dhika-rri        nguthi
give-factive 1sg.erg 2sg.acc boomerang  2sg.erg 1sg.acc give-go.back-irrealis meat
When/if I give back the boomerang, you will give me back the meat.
```

Here the speaker would like the listener to give back the meat, but there's no guarantee the listener will agree.

Q: What does the word *irrealis* mean?
A: ‘Unreal’: things that are not (or not yet) real or certain.

Q: What does {-rri} on {ngutya-rri} tell us about the giving?
A: It might happen, but it isn't guaranteed.

Q: Which ending in 199/7 shows what the speaker wishes would happen?
A: {-rri}, in {ngutya-dhika-rri}.

## Sure to happen: -lha
id: factive-lha
grammar: Verbs › Factive: -lha, p. 30

The *factive* ending {-lha} is the opposite of {-rri}: it shows the speaker is **certain** the event will happen. It is called *factive* because it's a fact that the thing will happen.

```ex 178/50
dhuna-bari ngali  wira-rra    burli-lha    nhani-guru   ngaka-ani
time-think we.nom wait-future fall-factive it.nom-there water-nominative
The two of us will wait until the water falls.
lit: The two of us will wait, the water will fall.
```

The speaker knows that the water will fall, so they use {-lha} on {burli} ‘fall’.

```ex part
wikarla  mirla warlpa-lha
tomorrow sun   rise-factive
Tomorrow when the sun comes up…
```

The speaker knows for a fact that the sun will come up tomorrow.

Q: What does the factive ending {-lha} show?
A: That the speaker is certain the event will happen.

Q: Why is {-lha} used on {burli} ‘fall’ in 178/50?
A: Because the speaker knows the water will fall.

Q: What does {wikarla mirla warlpa-lha} mean?
A: ‘Tomorrow when the sun comes up…’

## About to: -lha
id: factive-now
grammar: Verbs › Factive: -lha, p. 30

{-lha} has a second use: it can show that an action is happening right away, often translated as ‘now’. It's like English *about to*, as in *I'm about to run*.

```ex
mura guthi-lha    nganyi
okay run-factive I.nom
OK, I run (right now).
```

The first word, {mura} ‘OK’, also shows that the speaker is sure about what they are saying, so it fits well with {-lha}.

Q: What English phrase is like the second use of {-lha}?
A: ‘about to’.

Q: What does {mura} mean?
A: ‘OK’.

Q: Give the two uses of {-lha}.
A: Showing an event is certain to happen, and showing an action is about to happen right away.

## Could happen: -langu
id: possibility-langu
grammar: Verbs › Possibility: -langu, p. 31

The ending {-langu} is a lot like English *can* or *could*. It shows that something might or could happen, or that someone is able to do something. It is sometimes written {-langa}; we aren't sure yet whether these are two different endings, or one ending written in two ways.

{-langu} is similar in meaning to {-rri}, but {-rri} is used more for asking for things to happen, while {-langu} is more about someone being **able** to do something, or something being **possible**.

```ex 97/33
dratya-langa     nhandru-guru waliwa-nha
bite-possibility it.erg-there child-acc
It could bite that kid.
```

```ex 106/42
gatyi-li-ba-langu
break-apart-motion-possibility
(It) might break.
```

Something is able to bite, or to break, but it doesn't mean it will happen.

Q: Which English words is {-langu} like?
A: *can* and *could*.

Q: What is the difference between {-rri} and {-langu}?
A: {-rri} is used more for asking for things to happen; {-langu} is more about ability or possibility.

Q: What does {gatyi-li-ba-langu} mean?
A: ‘(It) might break.’

## Can't: walya with -langu
id: cannot
grammar: Verbs › Possibility: -langu, p. 31

When {-langu} is used with {walya} ‘not’, it means someone or something is **not able** to do something.

```ex 382/62
walya ngathu nhanha-guru   ganga-langu
not   I.erg  it.acc-there carry-possibility
I cannot carry this.
```

{ganga-langu} on its own would mean ‘can carry’; with {walya} it means ‘cannot carry’.

Q: What does {walya} mean?
A: ‘not’.

Q: How does {walya} change the meaning of {ganga-langu}?
A: From ‘can carry’ to ‘cannot carry’.

Q: What does {walya ngathu nhanha-guru ganga-langu} mean?
A: ‘I cannot carry this.’

## Verb endings at a glance
id: verb-summary
grammar: Verbs › Summary, p. 32

These three sentences are the same except for the verb ending:

```ex 258/2
garna-bula bula-guru     guthi-gala
man-dual   3dl.nom-there run-present
The two men run away.
```

```ex 259/3
garna-bula bula-guru     guthi-nga
man-dual   3dl.nom-there run-past
The two men ran away.
```

```ex 260/4
garna-bula bula-guru     guthi-rra
man-dual   3dl.nom-there run-future
The two men will run away.
```

| Verb form | Ending | For actions or events that… |
|---|---|---|
| present | {-gala} | are happening now |
| past | {-nga}, {-gali} | have already happened |
| future | {-rra} | will happen, at any time in the future |
| irrealis | {-rri} | might happen but aren't certain; things the speaker wishes would happen |
| factive | {-lha} | will certainly happen, or are about to happen |
| possibility | {-langu}, {-langa} | are possible |

Q: Which ending puts a verb in the future?
A: {-rra}.

Q: What does {garna-bula bula-guru guthi-nga} mean?
A: ‘The two men ran away.’

Q: Which ending would you use for something that might happen but isn't certain?
A: {-rri}.
