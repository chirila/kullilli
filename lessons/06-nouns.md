# Noun endings

Kullilli shows what each noun is doing in a sentence with endings. You don't need to remember the names of the endings: they are just labels for the different jobs.

## What is a noun?
id: what-is-a-noun
grammar: Nouns › What is a noun?, p. 33

A noun is a word that names something: usually people, places or things. Nouns can also name ideas, feelings, dreams and thoughts. This is true in every language. There are three groups:

- **Common nouns**: general words for people, places and things (person, dog, tree, food, house, anger), such as {garna} ‘man’, {dhithi} ‘dog’, {ngurra} ‘camp’.
- **Proper nouns**: names, which in English usually start with a capital letter (Australia, Kullilli, March).
- **Pronouns**: words used instead of nouns (you, she, he, them, us, it). They have their own units.

Q: What does a noun do?
A: It names something: a person, place, thing, or an idea or feeling.

Q: What are the three groups of nouns?
A: Common nouns, proper nouns and pronouns.

Q: Is ‘anger’ a noun?
A: Yes (a common noun naming a feeling).

## Endings show who does what
id: case-intro
grammar: Nouns › Noun Suffixes, p. 33

Take the English sentence *The child saw the dog*. We know the child did the seeing because in English the doer usually comes before the verb, and the one it's done to comes after. Swap them (*The dog saw the child*) and the meaning changes.

Kullilli shows each noun's role with an **ending** on the noun. Linguists call this *case*. So word order is not the main way Kullilli shows who does what. For example, {-andru} on a noun shows that it is the doer:

```ex 485/37
mara dhapa-gala  nganha dhithi-andru
hand lick-present me.acc dog-ergative
The dog is licking my hand.
```

Unlike English, the doer here comes at the end of the sentence, after the verb.

Q: How does English show who did what in *The child saw the dog*?
A: By word order: the doer comes before the verb.

Q: How does Kullilli show it?
A: With endings on the nouns (case).

Q: In 485/37, who is doing the licking, and how can you tell?
A: The dog: {dhithi-andru} has the doer ending {-andru}.

## The only noun: -ani
id: nominative-ani
grammar: Nouns › Nominative, p. 34

Take the English sentence *The dog is good*. It has only one noun, *the dog*: a subject, but no object (no noun that has something done to it). In a sentence like this, the noun is in the *nominative* case, which Kullilli marks with {-ani}.

```ex 76/12
nhani  dhithi-ani     dhali
it.nom dog-nominative good
This dog is good.
```

```ex 319/63
warlpa-gala  mirkirinyi-ani
rise-present moon-nominative
The moon is coming up.
```

Q: When is a noun in the nominative case?
A: When it is the subject of a sentence with no object.

Q: What is the nominative ending?
A: {-ani} (or {-ni}; see the next lesson).

Q: What does {warlpa-gala mirkirinyi-ani} mean?
A: ‘The moon is coming up.’

## -ni after a
id: nominative-ni
grammar: Nouns › Nominative, p. 34

The grammar says that when a word already ends in **a**, the nominative ending is {-ni} instead of {-ani}. {warliwa} ‘child’ ends in *a*, so it takes {-ni}:

```ex 152/24
ganguri-gala nhani-guru    warliwa-ni
cry-present  it.nom-there child-nominative
That child is crying.
```

```ex 141/13
ngaka-lunga    dhana-guru  warliwa-ni       winta-gala
water-locative them-there child-nominative swim-present
The kids are all swimming in the water.
```

This sentence has two nouns, the kids and the water, but the kids aren't doing anything **to** the water: they are **in** it. So {warliwa} still takes the nominative ending. (Talking about places has its own lessons.)

> You will also see {-ani} after words ending in *a* in some recorded sentences, such as {garna-ani} ‘man’ and {ngaka-ani} ‘water’.

Q: Which form of the nominative goes on {warliwa} ‘child’?
A: {-ni}: {warliwa-ni}.

Q: {walga} means ‘woman’. What is its nominative form?
A: {walga-ni}.

Q: Why don't the kids in 141/13 take a doer ending like {-andru}?
A: They aren't doing anything to the water; they are in it.

## Doing something to something: -andru
id: ergative-andru
grammar: Nouns › Ergative, p. 34–35

The *ergative* case is used for a subject when the sentence **also has an object**. In *The dog saw the child*, the dog does something to something else (the child), so in Kullilli it takes {-andru}:

```ex made-up
dhithi-andru nadya-nga waliwa-nha
dog-ergative see-past  child-accusative
The dog saw the child.
```

```ex 469/21
gubi-andru      nhanha-guru   dhali-munka-rra
doctor-ergative it.acc-there good-causative-future
The doctor will heal it.
lit: The doctor will make it good.
```

Q: When does a subject take {-andru}?
A: When the sentence also has an object.

Q: What's the difference between {dhithi-ani} and {dhithi-andru}?
A: {dhithi-ani}: the dog is the only noun (no object). {dhithi-andru}: the dog is doing something to something else.

Q: What does {gubi-andru nhanha-guru dhali-munka-rra} mean?
A: ‘The doctor will heal it’ (literally ‘will make it good’).

## Another doer ending: -lu
id: ergative-lu
grammar: Nouns › Ergative, p. 35

Another ending used the same way is {-lu}. But we only have examples of it with the word for ‘uncle’, the word for ‘who’, and the number ‘two’.

```ex 99/35
gawalya-lu     marnnga-gala bakaranyi
uncle-ergative make-present boomerang
Uncle is making a boomerang.
```

```ex 111/47
wara-lu      nganytya manhdha-nga nhangala makurra-nha
who-ergative my       grasp-past  3sg.loc  stick-accusative
Who picked up my stick here?
```

The recorded sentences don't clearly show the difference between {-andru} and {-lu}. It might have to do with gender: some other Aboriginal languages, like Diyari, have case endings that depend on gender, so {-lu} might be an optional ending for male nouns. We can't be sure.

Q: Which three words are {-lu} found with?
A: ‘uncle’, ‘who’ and ‘two’.

Q: What does {gawalya-lu marnnga-gala bakaranyi} mean?
A: ‘Uncle is making a boomerang.’

Q: Do we know for sure why there are two doer endings?
A: No. It might have to do with gender, as in Diyari, but we can't be sure.

## Having something done to it: -anha
id: accusative-anha
grammar: Nouns › Talking about things that have an action done to them (accusative), p. 35–36

In a sentence with two nouns, one does the action and the other has the action done to it. The second one is in the *accusative* case, marked with {-anha}.

```ex 13
nguthi-anha     ngandra-ngu dhalhtha-rra
meat-accusative we-ergative eat-future
All of us will eat the meat.
```

```ex 33
yundru  nadya-gala  dhaldra-anha
you.erg see-present kangaroo-accusative
You see the kangaroo.
```

Word order doesn't decide who does what. In 13 the noun with {-anha} comes before the verb; in 33 it comes after. Either way, {-anha} tells us it is the one the action is done to.

> The grammar says that, unlike {-ani}, {-anha} doesn't shorten after a word ending in *a*. But you will see {-nha} in some recorded sentences, such as {makurra-nha} ‘stick’.

Q: Which ending marks the noun that has the action done to it?
A: {-anha} (accusative).

Q: In 13, does the noun with {-anha} come before or after the verb?
A: Before the verb.

Q: How do you know {dhaldra-anha} is what is being seen, wherever it is in the sentence?
A: Because it has the accusative ending {-anha}.

## To someone, for someone: -anga
id: dative-anga
grammar: Nouns › Talking about a noun receiving something or benefitting from an action (dative), p. 36–37

A noun that receives something, or that an action is done for, is in the *dative* case, marked with {-anga} (sometimes {-nga}). It is like English *to (someone)* or *for (someone or something)*: *The child gave a toy to the dog.*

```ex 342/22
wanki-nga barninyi nganyi wanki-nga nhungka bakaranyi-anga
find-past unable   I.nom  find-past his     boomerang-dative
I looked, I couldn't find his boomerang.
```

Q: Which English words are like the dative ending?
A: ‘to’ and ‘for’.

Q: What is the dative ending?
A: {-anga} (sometimes {-nga}).

Q: Which word in 342/22 has the dative ending?
A: {bakaranyi-anga} ‘boomerang’.

## Going to, going for: -anga
id: dative-goal
grammar: Nouns › dative, p. 37

The dative can also show the **goal** of an action: where someone is going.

```ex 7
dhika-gala      nganytya ngurra-anga
go.back-present my       camp-dative
I'm going back to my camp now.
```

It can also show a **purpose**: what someone is going for.

```ex 130/2
nguthi-anga bula-guru      yanhtha-gala
meat-dative them.two-there go-present
The two of them are going for meat.
```

Q: What does {ngurra-anga} mean in 7?
A: ‘to the camp’.

Q: How do you say ‘going for meat’?
A: {nguthi-anga … yanhtha-gala}.

Q: What two kinds of goal can the dative show?
A: A place someone is going to, and a purpose (what they're going for).

## At, in, on: -langa, -lunga
id: locative
grammar: Nouns › Talking about where something is (locative), p. 37–38

To say where something is, English uses *at*, *in* and *on*. Kullilli uses the *locative* endings {-langa} or {-lunga}. There is no clear difference between them in the recorded sentences; {-lunga} is used more often.

```ex 161/33
gulha-rra      ini nganytya ngurra-langa
sit-imperative you my       camp-locative
Sit down in my camp.
```

```ex 357/37
mura ngathu nhangala-guru gali-nga daka-langa
OK   I.erg  it.loc-there  put-past ground-locative
OK, I put (it) over there on the ground.
```

Q: What are the two locative endings?
A: {-langa} and {-lunga}.

Q: How do you say ‘in my camp’?
A: {nganytya ngurra-langa}.

Q: What does {daka-langa} mean?
A: ‘on the ground’.

## At night, with the dog: more uses of -langa
id: locative-time-company
grammar: Nouns › locative, p. 38

The locative can also say **when** something happens:

```ex 455/7
niladha   nhani-guru   buka dhithi-ani     ngawu-langa   ngandrangala yanhtha-dhika-gali
yesterday it.nom-there all  dog-nominative night-locative us.loc       go-go.back-past
Yesterday those dogs came up to us at night.
```

And it can mean ‘in company with, with’:

```ex 540/26
nhungala-guru birkibirki-wa      dhali-lunga   dhithi-lunga
it.loc-there  play.play-emphatic good-locative dog-locative
That one down there is playing with the good dog.
```

Both words for ‘the good dog’, {dhali} ‘good’ and {dhithi} ‘dog’, have {-lunga}.

Q: How do you say ‘at night’?
A: {ngawu-langa}.

Q: What does {dhali-lunga dhithi-lunga} mean?
A: ‘with the good dog’.

Q: Name two meanings of the locative besides ‘at, in, on’.
A: A time (‘at night’) and company (‘with’).

## From: -andru
id: ablative
grammar: Nouns › Talking about movement away from something (ablative), p. 38–39

The endings {-andru} or {-ndru} on a noun show movement **away from** it. This is called the *ablative* case. It sounds the same as the ergative {-andru}, but it does a different job.

```ex 59
nhungka ngurra-andru nganytya ngarnidya-ni      dhika-gala
his     camp-ablative my      father-nominative go.back-present
My father comes back from his camp.
```

```ex 64
buli-nga burngu-andru      nhani
fall-past gum.tree-ablative it.nom
That one fell down from that gum tree.
```

The ablative is also how you say where someone is from: {ngaka-nuga-andru} ‘from the big water’, {Brisbane-andru} ‘from Brisbane’.

Q: What does {-andru} mean on {burngu} ‘gum tree’ in 64?
A: ‘from’.

Q: How can {-andru} mean both ‘doer’ and ‘from’?
A: They are two different endings that sound the same; the rest of the sentence shows which one it is.

Q: How do you say ‘from his camp’?
A: {nhungka ngurra-andru}.

## With a tool: -andru
id: instrumental
grammar: Nouns › Talking about using something (instrumental), p. 39

The same-sounding ending {-andru} (or {-ndru}) also shows the **tool** or thing used to do something. This is the *instrumental* case, like English *with* in *The child ate with a spoon*.

```ex 348/28
nampa    nhanha-guru  dhaldra-anha        dulpa-andru
cover.up it.acc-there kangaroo-accusative ashes-instrumental
Cover up this kangaroo with ashes.
```

```ex 86/22
galka-iyi-gali         nhani-guru   daranya-andru
strike-reflexive-past he.nom-there axe-instrumental
He hit himself with a stone axe.
```

Q: How do you say ‘with ashes’?
A: {dulpa-andru}.

Q: What does the instrumental case show?
A: The tool or thing used to do something.

Q: How many different jobs does {-andru} do?
A: Three: the doer (ergative), ‘from’ (ablative) and ‘with a tool’ (instrumental).

## Noun endings at a glance
id: case-summary
grammar: Nouns › Summary of Kullilli Cases & Noun Endings, p. 40

| Case | Ending | For nouns that are… |
|---|---|---|
| nominative | {-ani}, {-ni} | the subject of a sentence with no object |
| ergative | {-andru}, {-lu} | the subject of a sentence with an object |
| accusative | {-anha} | the object: the one the action is done to |
| dative | {-anga} | the goal, receiver or beneficiary of an action |
| locative | {-langa}, {-lunga} | the place where something happens |
| ablative | {-andru} | the place something moves away from |
| instrumental | {-andru} | the tool used to do something |

- The nominative is {-ani} in most cases, but the grammar gives {-ni} for nouns ending in *a*.
- The ergative is usually {-andru} but sometimes {-lu}, which might have to do with gender.
- The dative has several forms: {-anga}, {-nga}, {-angani}, {-ngani}. We're not sure yet how this works.

Q: Which ending would you put on a tool?
A: {-andru} (instrumental).

Q: Which endings show where something happens?
A: {-langa} and {-lunga} (locative).

Q: Name the forms of the dative ending.
A: {-anga}, {-nga}, {-angani} and {-ngani}.
