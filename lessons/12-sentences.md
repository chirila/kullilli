# Making sentences and asking questions

Now that you know about verbs, nouns and their endings, you can put sentences together.

## Sentences without a verb
id: no-verb
grammar: Putting sentences together, p. 70

In English, the simplest sentence has a noun and a verb (*I ate*). Sentences that say what something is use a form of *to be*: *I am a person*. Every full English sentence has a verb.

Kullilli has **no verb ‘to be’**, so the simplest Kullilli sentence can have no verb at all: just two words, one saying something about the other, with case endings to show how they relate.

```ex 506/58
miti-ani         ingka   mara-ani
dirty-nominative 2sg.dat hand-nominative
Your hand is dirty.
```

Q: Is there a Kullilli verb for ‘to be’?
A: No.

Q: What does {miti-ani ingka mara-ani} mean?
A: ‘Your hand is dirty.’

Q: Does every Kullilli sentence need a verb?
A: No. A sentence can be just two words, one describing the other.

## Word order is free
id: word-order
grammar: Putting sentences together, p. 70–71

English sentences usually go *subject, verb, object*, and that's how we know who does what: *The dog eats food* means something very different from *The food eats dog*.

Kullilli sentences don't depend on word order, because the endings show each noun's role:

```ex 217/25
yula-guru-ngu      dhalhtha-rra gunga-anha
2dl-there-ergative eat-future   food-accusative
The two of you eat this food.
```

Swapping the two nouns doesn't change the meaning, because {-ngu} and {-anha} still show who is eating and what is eaten:

```ex made-up
gunga-anha      dhalhtha-rra yula-guru-ngu
food-accusative eat-future   2dl-there-ergative
The two of you eat this food.
```

To give a word extra attention (*focus*), move it to the beginning of the sentence. There are probably some limits on word order, which are still being looked into.

Q: Does swapping the nouns in 217/25 change the meaning?
A: No. The endings {-ngu} and {-anha} still show who eats and what is eaten.

Q: How do you give a word extra attention?
A: Move it to the beginning of the sentence.

Q: In English, what tells you who does what in *The dog eats food*?
A: Word order.

## Yes-or-no questions
id: yes-no-questions
grammar: Asking Questions › Polar (“yes or no”) questions, p. 72

English often starts a yes-or-no question with *Do* or *Are* (*Are you enjoying learning Kullilli?*). In Kullilli it's simple: put the **verb at the beginning** of the sentence.

```ex 245/53
ganhdhuganga-gala  yundru  dhana-guru-anha
understand-present 2sg.erg 3pl-there-accusative
Do you understand them?
```

Also, raise the pitch of your voice at the end of the sentence, just as English speakers do in a question.

Q: How do you make a yes-or-no question in Kullilli?
A: Put the verb at the beginning, and raise your voice at the end.

Q: What does {ganhdhuganga-gala yundru dhana-guru-anha} mean?
A: ‘Do you understand them?’

Q: What happens to the pitch of your voice at the end of a question?
A: It rises.

## Questions with question words
id: wh-questions
grammar: Asking Questions › Information questions, p. 72–73

Questions asking *who*, *what*, *where*, *why* and so on are called *information questions*. In Kullilli, put the question word (see the Question words unit) **at the beginning** of the sentence.

```ex 111/47
wara-lu      nganytya manhdha-nga nhangala makurra-nha
who-ergative 1sg.dat  grasp-past  3sg.loc  stick-accusative
Who picked up my stick here?
```

```ex 327/7
mina-anga   nhina        dhithi-gala
what-dative 3sg.masc.nom laugh-present
Why (is he) laughing?
```

```ex 133/5
ngala-andru    ini
where-ablative 2sg.nom
Where are you from?
```

Q: Where does the question word go in a Kullilli question?
A: At the beginning.

Q: Which question word starts ‘Why is he laughing?’
A: {mina-anga}.

Q: Ask ‘Where are you from?’
A: {ngala-andru ini}.

## Joining sentences together
id: joining
grammar: Asking Questions, p. 73

To join related sentences in English, we use words like *and*. In Kullilli you don't need to: just say the sentences one after another and they are understood to go together.

```ex 113/49
ngathu  manhdha-nga wirnbi-nga ngathu  wiyi-lunga
1sg.erg grasp-past  throw-past 1sg.erg fire-locative
I picked it up and threw it in the fire.
```

The English translation needs *and*, but the Kullilli just puts the two actions next to each other.

Q: Is there a word for ‘and’ in 113/49?
A: No. The two parts are just said one after the other.

Q: What does {ngathu manhdha-nga} mean?
A: ‘I picked (it) up.’

Q: How do you join two related sentences in Kullilli?
A: Say them one after the other.
