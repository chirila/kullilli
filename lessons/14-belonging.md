# Owning and belonging

Kullilli has two different ways of saying *my*, *your* and *his/her*: one for things that can be separated from their owner, and one for things that can't, such as body parts.

## My and your: nganytya, ingka
id: my-your
grammar: Next steps › More on owning, possessing, and belonging › Alienable (separable) possession, p. 76–77

Most things that belong to someone can be separated from them. A toy can belong to one child, but if she gives it away, it belongs to someone else. Linguists call this *alienable* (separable) possession.

For this kind of belonging, ‘my’ or ‘mine’ is {nganytya} (or {nganytyani}).

```ex
barninyi nganyi wanki-nga nganytya makurrangani
unable   I.nom  find-past my       stick
I couldn't find my stick.
```

The stick belongs to the speaker, even if they can't find it, but it could become someone else's stick if they gave it away.

‘Your’ is {ingka}. English *your* can be about one person or many, but in Kullilli these are different words: use {ingka} only when talking to **one** person.

```ex 332/12
wani ingka yarinya-ani
what your  name
What's your name?
```

Q: How do you say ‘my’ for something that can be separated from you?
A: {nganytya}.

Q: When do you use {ingka} ‘your’?
A: When talking to one person.

Q: What does *alienable* possession mean?
A: Owning something that can be separated from the owner (it could be given away).

## His, her, their: nhungka
id: his-her
grammar: Next steps › More on owning, possessing, and belonging › Alienable (separable) possession, p. 77–78

English has *his*, *her* and *their*. Kullilli has just one word, {nhungka}, which doesn't show gender. It is translated as ‘his’ when the owner is male, ‘her’ when the owner is female, and ‘their’ when the speaker doesn't know.

```ex
nhungka ngurraandru nganytya ngarnidyani dhikagala
his     camp        my       father      go.back
My father comes back from his camp.
```

```ex
nhungka ngurraandru walgani dhikagala
her     camp        woman   go.back
The woman comes back from her camp.
```

```ex
nhungka ngurraandru bariani  dhikagala
their   camp        stranger go.back
The stranger comes back from their camp.
```

Some common things to talk about:

```words
nganytya ngarnidya | my father
ingka ngarnidya | your father
ingka dhithi | your dog
ingka yarinya | your name
nhungka yarinya | his name
```

Q: How many Kullilli words are there for ‘his’, ‘her’ and ‘their’?
A: One: {nhungka}.

Q: How do you say ‘your dog’ (to one person)?
A: {ingka dhithi}.

Q: How would you translate {nhungka} when the owner is a woman?
A: ‘her’.

## My hand: body parts
id: body-parts
grammar: Next steps › More on owning, possessing, and belonging › Inalienable (inseparable) possession, p. 78

Some things can't exist apart from their owner, like a person's spirit. This is *inalienable* (inseparable) possession. In Kullilli, **body parts** belong to this group, and a different word for ‘my’ is used. Compare:

```ex
nganytya dhithi mipa
my       dog    bad
My dog is bad.
```

```ex
mara-ani nganyi dhuka-gala
hand     my     swell-present
My hand is swelling.
```

Both {nganytya} and {nganyi} mean ‘my’, but {nganytya} is used for things that can be separated from the owner, and {nganyi} for things, like body parts, that can't. {nganyi} usually means ‘I’, but next to a noun it can mean ‘my’.

```ex
iliri balhti-gala nganyi
tears fall-present my
My tears are running.
```

```words
nganyi dhina | my foot
```

There are a few examples of {nganytya} with body parts too, such as {wikarla guka nganytya murta-rra} ‘Tomorrow my head will hurt’. We have very few examples like this, so it's hard to say why. Overall, {nganyi} is the usual way to say ‘my’ with a body part.

Q: Which ‘my’ is usually used with body parts?
A: {nganyi}.

Q: How do you say ‘my foot’?
A: {nganyi dhina}.

Q: What does {nganyi} usually mean, apart from ‘my’?
A: ‘I’.

## Your hand, his leg
id: body-parts-2
grammar: Next steps › More on owning, possessing, and belonging › Inalienable (inseparable) possession, p. 79

For ‘your (body part)’, use {ini} instead of {ingka}. Like {nganyi}, {ini} has two meanings: ‘you’ and ‘your’. This sentence is from Charlie Phillips:

```ex
minha munkaiyi-gali ini  mara-ani
what  happen-past   your hand
What happened to your hand?
```

For ‘his, her, its (body part)’, use {nhaniguru} instead of {nhungka}; it also means ‘he, she, it’.

```words
nhaniguru nantyinyanha | his shin
watyana nhaniguru | his leg
```

So for body parts, the words for ‘my, your, his/her’ are the nominative pronouns. The important thing to remember is that there are two ways to talk about things that belong to people:

| | Can be separated from the owner | Can't be separated (mostly body parts) |
|---|---|---|
| my | {nganytya} | {nganyi} |
| your | {ingka} | {ini} |
| his, her | {nhungka} | {nhaniguru} |

Q: How do you say ‘your hand’?
A: {ini mara-ani}.

Q: Which word means ‘his’ with a body part?
A: {nhaniguru}.

Q: Which ‘my’ goes with ‘dog’, and which with ‘hand’?
A: {nganytya} with ‘dog’, {nganyi} with ‘hand’.

## Mine, yours: -warra
id: warra-mine
grammar: Next steps › More on owning, possessing, and belonging › Belonging to: The suffix -warra, p. 79–80

The ending {-warra} means ‘belonging to’. On a possessive pronoun like {nganytya} ‘my’, it makes a word meaning ‘mine’:

```ex 166/38
nganytya-warra    ngathu  ngalka-nga nhanha  nguthi-anha
1sg.dat-belonging 1sg.erg think-past 3sg.acc meat-accusative
I thought this meat here was mine.
```

There are no recorded sentences with {-warra} on the other possessive pronouns, but the same pattern gives these forms (made up by the guide's authors):

```words
nganytya-warra | mine
ingka-warra | yours
nhungka-warra | his, hers
```

```ex made-up
ingka-warra       ngathu  ngalka-nga nhanha  nguthi-anha
2sg.dat-belonging 1sg.erg think-past 3sg.acc meat-accusative
I thought this meat here was yours.
```

Q: How do you say ‘mine’?
A: {nganytya-warra}.

Q: What does {-warra} mean?
A: ‘belonging to’.

Q: Is {ingka-warra} ‘yours’ found in the recorded sentences?
A: No. The authors made it up following the pattern of {nganytya-warra}.

## How many? When? -warra again
id: warra-questions
grammar: Next steps › More on owning, possessing, and belonging › Belonging to: The suffix -warra, p. 80–81

{-warra} has other uses too. It is on {wina} ‘close’ in {wina-warra} ‘close (to)’ (see the lesson on {wina}). And it is used to make questions about **quantity** and **time**.

{wandru} and {wanhdhu} both mean ‘much’, but {wandru} is about amounts of things and {wanhdhu} is about time. With {-warra}:

```words
wandru-warra | how much? how many?
wanhdhu-warra | when?
```

```ex 513/65
wanhdha-warra  dhalhdhra-ani
much-belonging kangaroo-nominative
How many kangaroos are there?
```

> In this recorded sentence the word is written {wanhdha-warra}, which looks more like the ‘time’ word, so this still needs checking.

Q: How would you ask ‘when?’
A: {wanhdhu-warra}.

Q: What is the difference between {wandru} and {wanhdhu}?
A: Both mean ‘much’; {wandru} is about amounts of things, {wanhdhu} about time.

Q: What does {wandru-warra} mean?
A: ‘how much? how many?’
