# Talking about time

Words like *yesterday*, *tomorrow* and *soon*. These words are relative to the day you say them, not exact days or times like *1 January* or *2 pm*.

## Yesterday: niladha
id: yesterday
grammar: More next steps › Relative time, p. 82

{niladha} means ‘yesterday’. Put it at the beginning or end of a sentence about something that happened the day before. These sentences were said by Charlie Phillips, who spoke Kullilli as his first language.

```ex
gunka-gali ngathu nhinha niladha
ask-past   I.erg  him    yesterday
I asked him yesterday.
```

```ex
niladha   gidani buka wagabala-gali nhangala
yesterday cattle all  lay-past      at.this.place
Yesterday all the cattle camped here.
```

Remember that the past ending {-gali} seems to be used when there's a time word like {niladha} in the sentence.

Q: What does {niladha} mean?
A: ‘yesterday’.

Q: Where in the sentence can {niladha} go?
A: At the beginning or the end.

Q: How do you say ‘I asked him yesterday’?
A: {gunka-gali ngathu nhinha niladha}.

## Today and tomorrow: bala, wikarla
id: today-tomorrow
grammar: More next steps › Relative time, p. 82–83

{bala} means ‘today’. At the end of a sentence it says the event happened, or will happen, on the same day.

```ex
walya nhulu dhalhtha-gali gungaanha bala
not   he    eat-past      food      today
He didn't eat any food today.
```

{wikarla} means ‘tomorrow’. Put it at the beginning or end of a sentence about something that will happen the next day.

```ex
nganytya ngarnidya dhika-gala wikarla
my       father    go.back-is tomorrow
My father is coming back tomorrow.
```

```ex
wikarla  guka nganytya murta-rra
tomorrow head my       hurt-future
Tomorrow my head will hurt.
```

One way to practise these words is to use them in English sentences with family and friends: instead of *we need to buy milk tomorrow*, say *we need to buy milk {wikarla}*.

Q: What does {bala} mean?
A: ‘today’.

Q: What does {wikarla} mean?
A: ‘tomorrow’.

Q: What does {walya nhulu dhalhtha-gali gungaanha bala} mean?
A: ‘He didn't eat any food today.’

## Two days away: -watyu
id: two-days
grammar: More next steps › Relative time, p. 83

Kullilli doesn't always need as many words as English. {wikarla-watyu} is one word, but in English it takes four: ‘the day after tomorrow’. It is {wikarla} ‘tomorrow’ with {-watyu} ‘one’: tomorrow plus one.

```ex
wikarla-watyu nganyi dhika-ra
tomorrow-one  I.nom  go.back-future
I will return the day after tomorrow.
```

In the same way, {niladha} ‘yesterday’ with {-watyu} makes {niladha-watyu} ‘the day before yesterday’. (This example was made up by the guide's authors.)

```ex made-up
niladha-watyu walya nhulu dhalhtha-gali gungaanha
yesterday-one not   he    eat-past      food
He didn't eat any food the day before yesterday.
```

Q: How do you say ‘the day after tomorrow’?
A: {wikarla-watyu}.

Q: How is {niladha-watyu} made, and what does it mean?
A: {niladha} ‘yesterday’ plus {-watyu} ‘one’; it means ‘the day before yesterday’.

Q: How many English words does it take to say {wikarla-watyu}?
A: Four: ‘the day after tomorrow’.

## Soon: gurnrtu
id: soon
grammar: More next steps › Relative time, p. 83; Temporal particles, p. 85

{gurnrtu} means ‘soon’: something that will happen in the near future. We don't know exactly how far in the future it can be used for, but it is a useful word.

```ex 318/62
gurnrtu-tyiri mirla-ani     warlpa-ba-rra
soon-?        sun-nominative rise-emphatic-future
Soon the sun will come up.
```

Q: What does {gurnrtu} mean?
A: ‘soon’.

Q: What does {gurnrtu-tyiri mirla-ani warlpa-ba-rra} mean?
A: ‘Soon the sun will come up.’

Q: Do we know exactly how far in the future {gurnrtu} can refer to?
A: No, not yet.

## Late and long ago: maltya, matya
id: late-long-ago
grammar: More next steps › Other ways to talk about time: Temporal particles, p. 84

Some other words mark points in time. They are sometimes called *temporal particles*.

{maltya} means ‘late’:

```ex 156/28
mirla-ani      maltya galguri-gala
sun-nominative late   set-present
The sun is going down – late.
```

The present ending {-gala} shows the sun is still going down, but later than expected.

{matya}, which sounds similar, means ‘long ago’:

```ex 391/7
matya    dhika-manytya-nga-ba         garna-bula
long.ago go.back-move-past-emphatic man-dual
Long ago two people went past.
```

Here the past ending {-nga} shows the event was over, and {matya} shows it happened a long time ago.

Q: What does {maltya} mean?
A: ‘late’.

Q: What does {matya} mean?
A: ‘long ago’.

Q: Why is {-nga} used in 391/7?
A: Because the event (two people going past) was completely over.

## Not yet, and a while: gadha, dhuna
id: not-yet-while
grammar: More next steps › Other ways to talk about time: Temporal particles, p. 84–85

{gadha} means ‘yet’. It is used with {walya} ‘not’ to mean ‘not yet’.

```ex 362/42
walya gadha ngathu  mulpa-gala
not   yet   1sg.erg finish-present
I haven't finished (it) yet.
```

{dhuna} means something like ‘a while’, usually about waiting for something:

```ex 390/6
dhuna-wira gurnrtu-nu dhana-guru bunhthi-na-rra
time-wait  soon-?     3pl-there  come-?-future
Wait a while, soon they will all come past.
```

This sentence also has {gurnrtu} ‘soon’.

Q: How do you say ‘not yet’?
A: {walya gadha}.

Q: What does {dhuna} mean?
A: ‘a while’ (usually about waiting).

Q: What does {walya gadha ngathu mulpa-gala} mean?
A: ‘I haven't finished (it) yet.’
