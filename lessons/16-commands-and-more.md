# Commands, each other, and ‘I think’

Telling people what to do, doing things to yourself or to each other, and showing you're not sure.

## Commands: just the verb
id: commands-bare
grammar: More next steps › Giving instructions, p. 85–86

It's useful to be able to give instructions. Kullilli has two ways to say commands. The first is to use the verb with **no ending**: none of {-gala}, {-gali}, {-nga} or {-rra}. A bare verb is understood as a command, much like English *go*, *stop*, *come*.

```ex
gawa nganala
come to.me
Come to me.
```

```ex
ngutya nganha
give   me.acc
Give me (it).
```

```ex
garki ingka dhithianha
call  your  dog
Call your dog off.
```

These commands don't include ‘you’, but you can include it:

```ex
nhanha nguthianha yundru dhalhtha
that   meat       you    eat
Eat that meat.
```

Q: What is the first way to make a command?
A: Use the verb with no ending.

Q: How do you say ‘Come to me’?
A: {gawa nganala}.

Q: Do you have to say ‘you’ in a command?
A: No, but you can.

## Commands with -rra
id: commands-rra
grammar: More next steps › Giving instructions, p. 86

The second way is to put {-rra} on the verb. Here are some common commands to practise every day:

```words
yanhdha-rra | go
gari-rra | stop
gulha-rra | sit
dhina-rra | stand
dhaltha-rra | eat
ngutya-rra | give
yanhdha-rra | speak
gunka-rra | ask
manhdha-rra | get (something)
manhdha-dhika-rra | bring (something)
walka-rra | put (something somewhere)
```

There are two different verbs written {yanhdha}: one means ‘go’ and the other means ‘speak’.

This ending looks just like the future {-rra}, and sometimes it may mean both. Think of a parent telling a child *You will stay home*: it's future, but it's also a command. These sentences could be read either way:

```ex 380/60
yundru gapa-rra      nganha
you    follow-future me.acc
You will follow me.
```

```ex
wiyianha yundru dhati-rra
firewood you    chop-future
You will chop firewood.
```

The guide's authors noticed that the bare command seems to be used more when talking to one person. Using {-rra} might also be more polite. We're not sure yet.

Q: What is the second way to make a command?
A: Add {-rra} to the verb.

Q: How do you say ‘sit!’ with {-rra}?
A: {gulha-rra}.

Q: Why can {yundru gapa-rra nganha} be a statement or a command?
A: Because the command ending {-rra} looks the same as the future ending {-rra}.

## Doing it to yourself: -iyi
id: reflexive
grammar: More next steps › Doing things to each other and oneself › -iyi reflexive, p. 87

When someone does something to themself, add {-iyi} to the verb. This is called a *reflexive* action. The subject pronoun is the nominative (such as {nganyi}), and the ‘myself’ or ‘himself’ is understood from {-iyi}.

```ex 87/23
galka-iyi-gali         nganyi
strike-reflexive-past 1sg.nom
I hit myself.
```

```ex 86/22
galka-iyi-gali         nhani-guru   daranya-andru
strike-reflexive-past he.nom-there axe-instrumental
He hit himself with a stone axe.
```

You have also seen {-iyi} in the feeling words {balhthi-iyi-gala} ‘I feel’ and {munka-iyi-gala} ‘I feel sick’.

Q: What does {-iyi} show?
A: That someone does something to themself (reflexive).

Q: How do you say ‘I hit myself’?
A: {galka-iyi-gali nganyi}.

Q: Which ‘I’ is used with {-iyi}: {nganyi} or {ngathu}?
A: {nganyi}.

## Doing it to each other: -nyara, -nyala
id: reciprocal
grammar: More next steps › Doing things to each other and oneself › -nyala/nyara: reciprocal, p. 87–88

When several people do the same thing to each other, so that each one both does it and has it done to them, add {-nyara} (or {-nyala}) to the verb. This is called a *reciprocal* action.

```ex 510/62
ngali   nadya-nyara-rra
1dl.nom see-reciprocal-future
The two of us will look at each other.
```

Each of the two looks, and is looked at.

```ex 343/23
yamba-nyara-gala          walga-ni         buka dhana-guru
fight-reciprocal-present woman-nominative all  3pl-there
That group of women are having a fight.
```

Each woman fights, and is fought by another in the group.

```ex 293/37
mura ngali   nadya-nyala-gali     niladha
OK   1dl.nom see-reciprocal-past yesterday
OK, the two of us were looking at each other yesterday.
```

Q: What does {-nyara} show?
A: That people do something to each other (reciprocal).

Q: How is {-nyara} different from {-iyi}?
A: {-iyi} is doing something to yourself; {-nyara} is doing it to each other.

Q: What does {ngali nadya-nyara-rra} mean?
A: ‘The two of us will look at each other.’

## I think…: bari
id: bari
grammar: More next steps › Showing Uncertainty: The verb bari ‘to think’, p. 88–89

In English you can show you're not sure by saying *I think…*, instead of *I know…* or just stating it. Kullilli does the same with {bari} ‘think’: add {bari} to any sentence to show you aren't certain it's true.

```ex 341/21
wanki-rra   bari  ngandra bakaranyi-anga
find-future think we.nom  boomerang-dative
I think we will look for (and find) your boomerang.
```

The speaker isn't sure they will find the boomerang.

```ex 439/55
yanhtha-rra bari  nganyi gubi-anga
go-future   think I.nom  doctor-dative
I will go to the doctor, I think.
```

```ex 372/52
walya bari  yanhtha-ba-nga yarlaguba
not   think go-away-past   somewhere
It hasn't gone away, I think.
```

Q: How do you show you aren't sure about something?
A: Add {bari} ‘think’ to the sentence.

Q: How would you say ‘You will go to the doctor, I think’?
A: {yanhtha-rra bari ini gubi-anga}.

Q: What does {walya bari yanhtha-ba-nga yarlaguba} mean?
A: ‘It hasn't gone away, I think.’
